The science behind the swarm

How do Desert
Locusts work?

A Desert Locust is a type of grasshopper; indeed, all types of locusts are grasshoppers, whilst not all grasshoppers are locusts. Desert Locusts fall under the category of Locusts, which is only about 20 of the 11–18 thousand species of grasshoppers that have been discovered.

Solitary vs. Gregarious Phases

Desert Locusts have two different "phases": solitary and gregarious. During their solitary phase, desert locusts tend to have cryptic colouration, larger eyes and antennae, a restricted diet, and live in low-density populations (usually fewer than 3 per 100 m²).

During their gregarious phase, desert locusts become much more active. They develop aposematic colouration, groom frequently, and adopt a much broader diet. Crucially, they swarm in groups exceeding 50 million locusts per km², devouring hundreds of metric tons of crops daily.

Swarm Formation and Impact

Desert Locusts gather in massive swarms that raid vast regions, devastating agricultural areas and triggering severe food shortages. Environmental triggers drive this transformation: heavy rains produce dense vegetation, drawing locusts together to feed.

This forced physical contact causes serotonin release in their nervous system, triggering rapid physical and behavioural shifts into their gregarious phase within a couple of hours.

An individual Desert Locust consumes roughly 2 grams of vegetation daily—about its own body weight. When multiplied across millions of insects in a single swarm, the impact on local agriculture and food security is catastrophic.
